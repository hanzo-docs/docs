# Policy and approvals

Some calls should not happen because a key was valid. Issuing a card, sending
mail to a customer, buying a domain, signing something: the credential says the
caller may act, and what is still missing is that somebody meant it.

Two products answer that, and they are two halves of one idea:

- **Policy** is the clauses. A tenant writes them, in order, and binds a subject
  to one exact revision of them.
- **Approvals** is where a held call waits. A person reads it, approves or denies
  it, and the call that was frozen is the call that runs.

> **API reference** · [Policy →](/docs/openapi/policy) — every endpoint, generated from the OpenAPI spec.

> **API reference** · [Approvals →](/docs/openapi/approvals) — every endpoint, generated from the OpenAPI spec.

Every address, argument and answer below lives in those two references, which are
generated from the same declarations that serve the calls. This page is the part
a reference cannot carry: what the two are for, and how they compose.

## Three effects, and the third one is the point

A clause earns an operation one of three effects — **allow**, **deny** or
**approve** — and there is no fourth.

Allow and deny are the ordinary two. **Approve** is the one that makes this
worth having: the call does not run and does not fail. It is *held*. The API
answers `202 Accepted` with a handle, the arguments are frozen exactly as they
were validated, and nothing happens until a person answers.

That is why an agent can be given a real credential. The dangerous half of what
it might do is not forbidden and not trusted — it is queued.

## What the caller sees

A held call answers `202`, which is a success status. A client that only asks
"did this fail?" would read a hold as a completed call and carry straight on with
nothing in its hands.

So the [TypeScript client](/docs/sdks/typescript) gives a gated call an answer
with two shapes — **done**, which carries the value, and **held**, which carries
the handle — and they share no field between them. A caller has to say which one
it got before it can reach anything, so a hold cannot be read as a success by
accident.

From the handle, a caller can wait for the answer rather than poll for it; that
is what the wait operation in the approvals reference is for.

## Clauses are read, not run

A clause is a pattern and an effect. The pattern is a literal operation id — the
same token the reference, the CLI, the tools and the SDK methods all call that
operation by — or a prefix ending in a wildcard segment. There is no expression
language, no regular expression and no script: a clause is a fact a person can
read out loud, and a revision does exactly what its lines say, top to bottom.

Order is the only precedence. The first clause whose pattern matches decides, and
a wildcard above a literal wins because the author put it there — nothing ranks
patterns behind the author's back. **An operation no clause matches is denied**:
a policy states what a subject may do, so silence is not permission.

## A revision is a value

Writing a policy that already exists does not edit it. It mints the next number
and leaves every earlier revision exactly as it was, and a subject is bound to
one **exact** revision — never to "the latest", because then publishing a
revision would silently change what governs someone.

That immutability is what makes an audit answerable. A decision names the
revision and the line that made it, in one spelling — `mail@3#2` — that a binding
uses, an approval freezes and a reader can go and look up months later.

## Governance is opt in, and then total

A subject nobody has bound is not decided here at all. They are outside this
authority's reach, not inside an empty policy that would refuse everything.

Once bound, they are governed completely — and if the revision that governs them
cannot be read, their calls are **denied**, because "governed by something I
cannot read" is not permission.

## Approving is a human act

Two refusals make an approval an approval, and neither of them is a clause a
tenant can write:

- A credential **acting for** somebody may read the queue and may wait on it. It
  may not answer it.
- Whoever raised a hold may not be the one who clears it. One refusal for both,
  so it holds for an operator and for a customer alike.

An approval that can be granted by whoever wanted it is not an approval.

## What is frozen, and what a person reads

The hold keeps the operation: who asked, what was asked for, the arguments as the
API validated them, and the digest they hashed to. Approving replays *those*
arguments — the ones the approver read — and there is nowhere in the approve call
to send a different set. Exactly once: a second approve reads the first one's
answer and runs nothing.

The arguments themselves are not part of any answer. A person reads a redacted
brief instead: the field names, the values needed to judge, and a
[KMS](/docs/services/kms) path where a credential would be. A secret is never a
value in a hold — a hold whose arguments carry one is refused rather than written
down.

## Who decides who

Policy decides **what may run**. It does not decide who you are, mint anything,
hold a session, or know a scope vocabulary of its own — that is
[IAM](/docs/iam)'s job alone, and policy reads the tenant, subject and actor IAM
minted.

Nor is it [Authz](/docs/authz), which is the other question and the older one:
authz asks whether a subject may act on a **resource** and answers allow or deny.
Policy asks whether a subject may run an **operation**, and can answer *hold*.

## Every surface, from one declaration

An operation is declared once, next to the code that serves it, and the REST
route, the OpenAPI entry, the tool the MCP door answers to, the CLI subcommand
and the method in every SDK are all projections of that one declaration. So each
entry in the two references above prints its own operation id, its SDK method
and — where the CLI and the door already carry it — the command and the tool
name, all resolved from that id rather than typed on this page.

Which is also why a clause's pattern is an operation id: the name a clause is
written against is the name the API publishes, so a clause and an operation cannot
drift apart.
