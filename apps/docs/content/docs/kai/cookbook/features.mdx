**Each question you ask Kai about a text is a number: a probability for a yes
or no, an expected level for a score.** Six such numbers per support
conversation, fed to a ridge regression, predicted the raters' churn risk
better than the mean did (test RMSE 0.546 against 0.625). Kai's own answer to
the harness's churn question did better than both (0.493).

## The idea

A model that must predict a number from text needs numbers to learn from. Ask
Kai the questions a person would weigh ("is the customer upset?", "have they
threatened to leave?") and use the answers as features: P(yes) for a noul, the
expected level Σ i·pᵢ for a score. A small regressor then learns how much each
matters for your target, from your labels.

The labelled data is the harness's `typed_decisions` suite: its 100
customer-service conversations, each rated by a panel for churn risk on four
levels. The target is the raters' mean level, 0 to 3. The script fits on 60
conversations and tests on the other 40, and compares four predictors:

- the mean of the training labels;
- Kai's answer to the harness's own churn question, as answered and refit with
  a line;
- the six features;
- the six features and the churn answer together.

## The code

```python
FEATURES = {
    "upset": Noul(
        instructions="The customer is upset.",
        criteria={"true": "they sound angry, frustrated or anxious", "false": "they sound calm or satisfied"},
    ),
    "leave": Noul(
        instructions="The customer threatens to leave.",
        criteria={
            "true": "they mention cancelling, switching, a chargeback, a dispute or a bad review",
            "false": "no such threat",
        },
    ),
    "again": Noul(
        instructions="The problem has happened before.",
        criteria={
            "true": "they say it keeps happening, or was reported before and is still not fixed",
            "false": "this is the first report",
        },
    ),
    "money": Noul(
        instructions="The customer has lost money.",
        criteria={
            "true": "a wrong, duplicate or unexpected charge, or a refund that has not arrived",
            "false": "no money is at stake",
        },
    ),
    "effort": Score(
        instructions="How much effort has the customer spent on this problem?",
        criteria=["almost none", "one message", "several messages or attempts", "repeated contacts over days"],
    ),
    "tone": Score(
        instructions="How does the customer's last message sound?",
        criteria=["pleased", "neutral", "annoyed", "furious"],
    ),
}
```

## What the numbers say

- **The features carry signal.** Six answers per conversation cut the test
  error from 0.625 to 0.546 and correlate 0.51 with the raters.
- **Two features do the work.** A threat to leave (+0.207 per standard
  deviation) and the tone of the last message (+0.240) carry the fit; upset,
  repeat, money and effort add almost nothing once those two are in.
- **A question Kai was trained on beats features built around it.** The
  harness's churn question, answered as is, has the lowest error (0.493) and
  the highest correlation (0.61). Refitting it on 60 labels made it slightly
  worse (0.517), and adding it to the features did not reach it (0.535).
- **Forty test conversations is a small test.** Treat the order of the last
  four rows as provisional, and measure on more of your own labels before you
  choose between them.

## Adapt it

- Use features when no question asks for your target directly: a revenue
  forecast, a time to resolution, a score your own raters give.
- Write each feature as one observable thing, a noul with criteria for both
  sides or a score with described levels, and let the regressor weigh them.
- Keep the answers as probabilities and expected levels; rounding them to yes
  and no throws away what the regressor learns from.
- Refit when your labels drift, and hold out conversations the fit never sees.
