title: Check citations
description: "Check each claim against the sentence it cites with one three-way question, and look in the whole document when the cited sentence says nothing."
---

**An answer that cites its source can cite a sentence that says nothing about
the claim, or one that says the opposite.** One question per claim tells the
three cases apart: the cited sentence supports the claim, contradicts it, or
does not address it. On twelve labelled claims Kai got eight right. Two of its
four misses called a contradicted claim supported, both at low confidence.

## The idea

Checking a citation is natural-language inference: the cited sentence is the
premise, the claim is the hypothesis, and the answer is one of three relations.
Kai's training includes that task in exactly this shape, a `premise`, a
`hypothesis` and the labels `entailment`, `neutral` and `contradiction`, so the
script asks it that way and renames the answers in code: entailment is
supported, contradiction is contradicted, neutral is absent.

When the cited sentence says nothing, the claim may still be true and only
miscited. The script asks once more with the whole document as the premise.

The document is a six-sentence returns policy we wrote for the recipe. The
twelve claims are what an assistant might say about it, each labelled twice:
against the sentence it cites, and against the whole policy.

## The code

```python
# a citation check is natural-language inference: the source is the premise, the claim the hypothesis
CHECK = Choice(
    instructions="How does `hypothesis` relate to `premise`?",
    criteria={
        "entailment": "the premise implies the hypothesis",
        "neutral": "the premise neither implies nor contradicts the hypothesis",
        "contradiction": "the premise contradicts the hypothesis",
    },
)
VERDICT = {"entailment": "supported", "neutral": "absent", "contradiction": "contradicted"}


def check(kai: Kai, source: str, claim: str) -> tuple[str, float]:
    """Kai's verdict on one claim against one source, with its confidence."""
    answer = kai.decide(state={"premise": source, "hypothesis": claim}, questions={"check": CHECK}).choices["check"]
    return VERDICT[answer.choice], answer.confidence

```

A claim whose cited sentence comes back `absent` is checked again against the
whole document:

```python
        verdict, confidence = check(kai, DOC[cite], claim)
        cited += verdict == gold
        line = f"{claim:{WIDTH}} {cite:5}  {gold:13} {verdict:13} {confidence:.2f}"
        if verdict == "absent":  # the cited sentence misses the claim: look in the whole document
            widened += 1
            verdict, confidence = check(kai, " ".join(DOC), claim)
            whole += verdict == truth
            line += f"  document: {verdict} {confidence:.2f} (gold {truth})"
```

## What the numbers say

- **The misses that matter are two negations.** "A sale item can be refunded"
  cites "Sale items can be exchanged but not refunded", and "The store pays for
  returns sent from abroad" cites "sent at the customer's expense". Kai called
  both supported, at 0.14 and 0.22.
- **One miscitation was called a contradiction.** "Refunds are made within 5
  business days" cites the gift card sentence, which says nothing about
  refunds' timing; Kai answered contradicted (0.45), so the claim never reached
  the whole-document pass.
- **Where the sentence was missed, the second pass recovered.** "Gift cards are
  final" came back absent against its own sentence and supported against the
  whole policy; "Sale items can be swapped" was found supported elsewhere, a
  true claim with the wrong citation.
- **The second pass has its own miss**: "Returns can be dropped off at any
  store" is in no sentence of the policy, and Kai called it contradicted (0.30).
- **Low confidence marks most misses, and some right answers.** Three of the
  four misses were at 0.22 or below; so was one right answer (0.19), and the
  fourth miss was at 0.45. A review line at 0.25 would have held three misses
  and one right answer, but it is drawn after seeing these twelve: set yours on
  labelled claims of your own.

## Adapt it

- Split a generated answer into claims and the sentences they cite, then ask
  one `check` per claim. Claims share nothing, so their calls can run in
  parallel.
- Keep the premise and hypothesis field names and the three relation labels;
  rename them in code, as `VERDICT` does.
- Treat a supported verdict on a claim that negates or reverses its source with
  suspicion: that is where this checkpoint erred. Put claims with "not",
  "only", "always" and "never" through review until your own labels say
  otherwise.
- Re-ask an absent claim against the whole source, as long as the source fits
  what Kai reads (1,024 tokens per question on this checkpoint); a longer
  source needs its likely passages chosen first.
